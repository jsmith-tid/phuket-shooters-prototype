I’d make this a case study about designing a better visit-planning journey for a mostly mobile tourist audience, rather than a tour of every page. It gives you a strong story about visual design, interaction design and business judgment.
The strongest material is:
1. Mobile hierarchy and enquiry flow. You treated the reported “about 95% mobile” audience as a design constraint: prominent prices and booking actions, a readable one-column form, full-width controls and practical information in the order a visitor needs it. Show an iPhone-width before/after and explain why the form moved ahead of “Before you visit.” Be candid that the date and time controls still need validation on iOS Safari.
2. Helping tourists decide and arrive. The homepage brings activities, supervision, reviews and visit information into a clearer sequence. The Find Us page embeds the map and makes contact details more compact. A simple journey diagram—choose an activity → check trust and practicalities → enquire → find the range—would show the interaction thinking behind the screens.
3. Serving a second audience without taking over the homepage. The compact English, Chinese and Thai tour-operator strip is a particularly good business-design example. You gave operators a direct route while keeping “Ways to enjoy” focused on individual visitors. Explain why the linked pages are labelled as demo placeholders and why coach parking and group-capacity claims await confirmation.
For visual-design evidence, show a small set of close comparisons: homepage hierarchy at mobile and desktop widths, the aligned gallery images, corrected head-and-shoulders team portraits, and the restrained reviews and affiliation treatments. Annotate why you changed scale, crop, spacing or prominence; polished screenshots alone won’t convey your decisions.
Be precise about research and results. The content audit, stakeholder feedback, reported mobile traffic mix and browser checks are real inputs. The Google rating is a trust signal, not a project outcome. Click events are prepared, but no analytics service is connected; the booking form does not submit. So frame conversion and usability gains as hypotheses to test, not achieved results. Useful next measures would be mobile task success, time to find prices or directions, and clicks from the operator strip by language.
Finally, state your role plainly: you set the requirements, priorities and design direction, reviewed iterations, and used Codex to implement the prototype. A good portfolio account makes those decisions, constraints and your contribution visible alongside the final UI—consistent with Nielsen Norman Group’s case-study guidance.
